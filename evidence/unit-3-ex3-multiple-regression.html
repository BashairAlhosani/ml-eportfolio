Coefficient¶

The coefficient is a factor that describes the relationship with an unknown variable. In this case, we can ask for the coefficient value of weight against CO2, and for volume against CO2. The answer(s) we get tells us what would happen if we increase, or decrease, one of the independent values.

In [2]:
print(regr.coef_)

[0.00755095 0.00780526]

The result array represents the coefficient values of weight and volume.

Weight: 0.00755095 Volume: 0.00780526

These values tell us that if the weight increase by 1kg, the CO2 emission increases by 0.00755095g.

And if the engine size (Volume) increases by 1 cm3, the CO2 emission increases by 0.00780526 g.

I think that is a fair guess, but let test it!

We have already predicted that if a car with a 1300cm3 engine weighs 2300kg, the CO2 emission will be approximately 107g.

What if we increase the weight with 1000kg (from 2300 to 3300) what will be the CO2 emission?

Ans: 107.2087328 + (1000 * 0.00755095) = 114.75968

Experiment 6 – Change a few data points¶

(a) Add one unusual car: light (1000 kg), large engine (3000 cm³), high CO2 (150 g/km). (b) Remove the three highest-emitting cars.

def fit_cars(data, label):
    m = linear_model.LinearRegression().fit(data[["Weight", "Volume"]], data["CO2"])
    return {"experiment": label, "n": len(data), "coef Weight": m.coef_[0], "coef Volume": m.coef_[1],
            "R²": m.score(data[["Weight", "Volume"]], data["CO2"]),
            "pred CO2 (1300 kg, 1300 cm3)": m.predict(pd.DataFrame({"Weight": [1300], "Volume": [1300]}))[0]}

odd = pd.DataFrame({"Car": ["Unusual"], "Model": ["test"], "Volume": [3000], "Weight": [1000], "CO2": [150]})
rows = [fit_cars(df, "original"),
        fit_cars(pd.concat([df, odd], ignore_index=True), "(a) + one unusual car"),
        fit_cars(df.drop(df.CO2.nlargest(3).index), "(b) without the 3 highest CO2 cars")]
pd.DataFrame(rows).round(5)

Findings – multiple linear regression¶

The original model is CO2 = 79.7 + 0.00755 × Weight + 0.00781 × Volume. It reproduces the notebook's predictions exactly: 107.21 g/km for 2300 kg and 114.76 g/km for 3300 kg.

What the experiments showed

  1. The predictions in the original notebook are extrapolations. The heaviest car in the data weighs 1,746 kg, so the 2,300 kg and 3,300 kg predictions are well outside anything the model has seen. The model assumes CO2 keeps rising in a straight line with weight, and nothing in the data supports that.
  2. The model explains only a small part of CO2. R² = 0.38, so about 62 % of the variation in CO2 comes from factors not in the model (fuel type, engine technology, aerodynamics, etc.).
  3. The two predictors overlap. Weight and Volume are correlated (r = 0.75). Each alone gives R² = 0.31 (Weight) or 0.35 (Volume), and together only 0.38. Most of what Weight explains, Volume explains too.
  4. Raw coefficients can't be compared. Converting Volume to litres changed its coefficient from 0.0078 to 7.81, while predictions and R² stayed the same. After standardising, a 1 SD increase in Volume adds about 3.0 g/km and a 1 SD increase in Weight adds about 1.8 g/km. Engine size matters more, even though the raw coefficients looked almost equal.
  5. The coefficients are unstable. Adding one unusual car (light, big engine, high CO2) flipped the Weight coefficient from +0.0076 to −0.0099, which would mean heavier cars emit less. R² still rose to 0.56. Removing the 3 highest-emitting cars cut the Volume coefficient by more than half. With only 36 cars and correlated predictors, the individual coefficients shouldn't be over-interpreted.

Ethical and professional note. If a model like this were used to estimate emissions for regulation or taxation, extrapolating to vehicles outside the data and over-trusting unstable coefficients could produce unfair or misleading figures. The data's range and limitations should be reported alongside every prediction.
