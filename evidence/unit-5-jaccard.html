Unit 5 e-Portfolio Activity – Jaccard Distance/Dissimilarity Calculations¶

Task: the table shows the pathological test results for three individuals. Calculate the Jaccard distance/dissimilarity for the pairs (Jack, Mary), (Jack, Jim) and (Jim, Mary).

This notebook works through the calculation by hand, then checks it with Python, interprets the result, and tests how much the answer depends on how the data is encoded.

Step 1 – Choose the attributes and encode them¶

The Jaccard measure is designed for asymmetric binary attributes. These are yes/no attributes where only the "yes" (positive) outcome carries information, such as having a symptom or a positive test. Two patients are not similar just because they both lack a rare symptom.

Attribute Type Decision
Gender symmetric binary: M and F are equally informative left out (Jaccard is not suited to it)
Fever, Cough asymmetric binary (symptom present or not) Y = 1, N = 0 (Cough uses P = present)
Test-1 to Test-4 asymmetric binary (test result) P (positive) = 1, N (negative) = 0

Assumption about "A". The activity doesn't define the value A in Test-2 and Test-4. It is treated here as absent, i.e. a negative result (A = 0). With this reading, the answers match the well-known textbook version of this example (Han, Kamber & Pei, 2012). Step 5 tests the alternative reading, where A means abnormal (A = 1).

Step 2 – The formula¶

For two patients i and j, compare them attribute by attribute and count:

j = 1 j = 0
i = 1 q (both positive) r (only i positive)
i = 0 s (only j positive) t (both negative)

$$\text{Jaccard distance } d(i, j) = \frac{r + s}{q + r + s} \qquad\qquad \text{Jaccard similarity} = \frac{q}{q + r + s} = 1 - d(i, j)$$

The negative matches (t) are ignored. This is what separates Jaccard from simple matching, which divides by q + r + s + t.

Step 4 – Check with Python¶

In [3]:
PAIRS = [("Jack", "Mary"), ("Jack", "Jim"), ("Jim", "Mary")]

def counts(a, b):
    a, b = np.asarray(a, dtype=bool), np.asarray(b, dtype=bool)
    return int(np.sum(a & b)), int(np.sum(a & ~b)), int(np.sum(~a & b)), int(np.sum(~a & ~b))

def jaccard_table(data):
    rows = []
    for i, j in PAIRS:
        q, r, s, t = counts(data.loc[i], data.loc[j])
        rows.append({"pair": f"({i}, {j})", "q": q, "r": r, "s": s, "t": t,
                     "calculation": f"({r} + {s}) / ({q} + {r} + {s})",
                     "Jaccard distance": (r + s) / (q + r + s),
                     "Jaccard similarity": q / (q + r + s),
                     "scipy check": jaccard(data.loc[i].astype(bool), data.loc[j].astype(bool))})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("pair")

results = jaccard_table(binary)
results.round(3)

Interpretation¶

  • Jack and Mary are the most similar (d = 0.33). They share both of Jack's positive findings (fever and a positive Test-1) and differ only on Test-3.
  • Jim and Mary are the least similar (d = 0.75). They share only the fever, which all three patients have.
  • Jack and Jim are in between (d = 0.67).

If these results reflect the underlying illness, Jack and Mary are the most likely to have a similar condition, and Jim and Mary the least likely. This is the same conclusion as the textbook example (Han, Kamber & Pei, 2012).

Step 6 – Why Jaccard, and not simple matching?¶

The same three patients are compared with two other common measures:

  • Simple matching (SMC) on the same 6 binary attributes, which also counts the negative matches t: d = (r + s) / (q + r + s + t).
  • Nominal matching on all 7 attributes, including Gender. Each value is treated as a category (N and A are different), and d = number of mismatches / 7.

  • Simple matching gives the same ranking here, but every distance is smaller because shared negatives count as agreement. With many tests and rare positive results, almost every patient would look "similar" to every other.
  • Nominal matching that includes Gender makes Jack and Jim the closest pair (0.29). They are both male and share several negative results, but they have only one positive finding in common. A sensitive, symmetric attribute (gender) and shared absences end up driving the result.

This shows why the distance measure has to match the type of data. Asymmetric medical findings call for Jaccard.

Ethical and professional considerations¶

  • Similarity is not diagnosis. A small distance only means two people have similar recorded findings. Any clinical conclusion needs medical judgement and far more than six attributes and three patients.
  • Health data is special-category personal data under the UK GDPR. Any real analysis needs a lawful basis, minimisation (use only the attributes needed) and protection against re-identification.
  • Encoding choices are analytical decisions. Leaving out Gender, and treating "A" as absent, both changed the result. These decisions should be documented and justified, not hidden in code.

Reflection¶

The arithmetic is simple. The real decisions came before it: which attributes to include, which values count as "positive", and whether shared absences should count as similarity. Changing just one of these (the meaning of "A", or including Gender) changed which patients appeared most alike. In clustering, every result depends on the distance measure, so understanding the data and documenting these choices matters more than the calculation itself.
