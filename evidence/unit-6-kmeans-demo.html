Customer Segmentation with K-Means¶

Imagine that you have a customer dataset, and you need to apply customer segmentation on this historical data. Customer segmentation is the practice of partitioning a customer base into groups of individuals that have similar characteristics. It is a significant strategy as a business can target these specific groups of customers and effectively allocate marketing resources. For example, one group might contain customers who are high-profit and low-risk, that is, more likely to purchase products, or subscribe for a service. A business task is to retaining those customers. Another group might include customers from non-profit organizations. And so on.

Part 2 – Review: the scaling bug, and the missing values¶

What goes wrong in Part 1¶

The demo standardises the data (Clus_dataSet = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)) and explains why this matters, but every model is then fitted on the unscaled X (k_means.fit(X), and both the elbow and silhouette loops). k-means uses Euclidean distance, so the feature with the largest spread dominates. Findings – the demo, reviewed¶

As written (unscaled) Scaling fixed Scaling fixed, without Defaulted
Segment sizes 649 / 183 / 18 537 / 166 / 147 489 / 286 / 75
What separates the segments income only (average 31k, 84k, 227k) default history, then age, income and debt age, income and debt
Silhouette 0.56 0.29 0.23
Agreement with the as-written version (ARI) – 0.30 0.37

1. The scaling bug changes the whole answer. The demo prepares standardised data but clusters the raw values. Income's spread is about 42 times larger than education's, so the "segments" are just three income bands. Fixing one variable name gives a completely different segmentation (ARI 0.30).

2. A higher silhouette is not a better segmentation. The buggy version scores best (0.56), because splitting along one dominant variable creates neat bands. The silhouette measures how well the clusters are separated, not whether they mean anything. On correctly scaled data, the silhouette is low for every k (0.35 at k = 2, and lower for every larger k) and the elbow is gradual. These customers don't form natural groups; any segmentation is a practical partition that the business must justify.

3. One binary column took over. After scaling, the 0/1 Defaulted flag creates a large gap, and one segment became almost entirely defaulters: 164 of its 166 customers had defaulted, while the largest segment (537 customers) contains none. The scaled segments are:

  • Defaulted, young, low income (166): age 32, income 32k, high debt-to-income (14).
  • Established, higher income (147): age 43, 17 years in work, income 102k, highest debts.
  • Young, low income, low debt (537): age 34, income 36k, debt-to-income 8, no recorded defaults.

4. Missing values were silently guessed. np.nan_to_num turned 150 unknown default statuses (18 % of customers) into "never defaulted". Those customers were then placed in the lower-risk segments without any evidence. Leaving the column out (version 3) avoids the guess, and still produces a recognisable older, high-income, heavily indebted segment (75 customers, debt-to-income 18).

Ethical and professional considerations. Using credit-default history to segment customers for marketing affects how people are treated (who gets offers, credit or worse terms). It is personal financial data under the UK GDPR, needs a lawful basis, and must be handled fairly and transparently. Because this segmentation depends so heavily on preprocessing choices (scaling, the missing-value rule, which columns to include), each choice must be documented and justified before the segments are used for decisions about real people.
