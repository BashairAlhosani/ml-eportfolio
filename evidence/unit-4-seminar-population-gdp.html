Unit 4 Seminar – Part 2: Population vs GDP per capita¶

Task A – Correlation. Pre-process the data: calculate the mean population of each country and the mean GDP per capita (2001–2021), handling the missing values. Investigate the correlation between them, interpret the plot and evaluate the Pearson correlation coefficient.

Task B – Regression. Linear regression with mean population as the independent variable and mean GDP per capita as the dependent variable.

Data: World Bank 4.Unit04 Global_Population.csv (total population) and 4.Unit04 Global_GDP.csv (GDP in current US$).

Three things to sort out before any analysis:

  1. The GDP file holds total GDP, not GDP per capita. Per capita has to be calculated for each year as GDP ÷ population. This is why the task needs both files.
  2. The GDP file ends in 2020 while population goes to 2021. GDP per capita can therefore only be calculated for 2001–2020. Mean population uses the full 2001–2021 range, as the task asks.
  3. Footnote and blank rows at the bottom of the population file, and regional groups in both files, must be removed (next step).

2. Pre-processing¶

2a. Remove regional and income-group aggregates¶

World Bank files mix countries with groups such as World, Euro area, High income and Sub-Saharan Africa. These aren't countries, and some are made of other rows (World = all countries), so they would distort the analysis. They are removed using the World Bank's aggregate codes.

How the missing values are handled

  • Gaps inside a series (a year missing between two known years) would be filled by linear interpolation, because population and GDP change smoothly. The check below shows there are none in this data: every missing value is at the start or end of a series. Examples are countries that only began reporting later (Kosovo, independent in 2008; Curaçao and Sint Maarten, separate countries since 2010), countries whose reporting stopped (Eritrea after 2011, Venezuela after 2014, South Sudan after 2015), and countries whose 2020/2021 figures weren't published yet.
  • Values at the start or end are not invented, because extrapolating GDP would be guesswork. The mean uses the years that are available.
  • A country is kept only if it has data for at least half the years (≥ 11 of 21 for population, ≥ 10 of 20 for GDP per capita), so that no "mean" is based on just a few years. Countries with no data at all are dropped.

The countries affected are listed below, so the choice is transparent.

Evaluation on unseen data and an alternative model¶

As in the demo, the model is also tested on an 80/20 train/test split. It is then compared with a model fitted on the log values (log GDP per capita vs log population), which suits the skewed data better.

Findings and results to discuss in the seminar¶

Pre-processing¶

  • GDP per capita had to be calculated (GDP ÷ population for each year), because the GDP file holds total GDP.
  • 49 regional and income-group aggregates were removed, leaving 217 countries and territories.
  • Year range: mean population covers 2001–2021. Mean GDP per capita covers 2001–2020, because the GDP file has no 2021 column.
  • Missing values: there were 16 missing population values and 216 missing GDP values, all at the start or end of a series and none in between. Means use the years available. 9 countries with fewer than half the years were dropped, 5 of them with no GDP data at all (e.g. North Korea and Gibraltar). The final dataset has 208 countries.

Task A – Correlation¶

Measure Value p-value

Interpreting the plot. The linear-scale scatter plot is "L-shaped". Almost every country is crowded into the bottom-left corner. The richest countries per person (Monaco, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, Bermuda) all have very small populations, and the two largest countries (China and India) have below-average GDP per capita. No straight line describes this pattern.

Evaluating the Pearson coefficient. r = −0.067 is almost zero and not statistically significant (p = 0.34). Population explains only 0.45 % of the differences in GDP per capita. The result is also a poor fit for Pearson's assumptions:

  • Both variables are extremely right-skewed (skewness 8.8 for population and 2.8 for GDP per capita), and the relationship is not linear.
  • On a log scale and with rank correlation, a weak negative relationship appears (about −0.3, now significant). Smaller countries tend to be slightly richer per person, mainly because of wealthy micro-states and financial centres.
  • Even this weak link is only an association. It doesn't mean a smaller population causes higher income.

Task B – Regression¶

Mean GDP per capita = 15,886 − 0.0000117 × mean population (about −11.7 US$ for every extra million people).

Value
R² 0.0045
p-value of the slope 0.34 (not significant)
Mean absolute error 15,869 US$
Test R² (80/20 split) −0.065, no better than always predicting the average (−0.064)
  • The regression line is almost flat. It predicts about 15,900 US$ for nearly every country, because population carries almost no information about income per person.
  • The residuals are huge and unevenly spread, which breaks the assumptions of linear regression.
  • A log-log model fits the shape of the data better, but still doesn't predict unseen countries better than the average.

Agreed conclusion for the seminar. Population size is not a useful predictor of GDP per capita, either by correlation or by linear regression. Income per person depends on other factors, such as productivity, education, institutions, natural resources and a country's role as a financial centre. A model that uses population alone would be misleading.

Limitations and ethical points¶

  • GDP is in current US$, not adjusted for inflation or price levels (PPP), so averages over 20 years mix different price levels.
  • Countries with partial data (e.g. South Sudan, 2008–2015) have means taken over different periods, so they are not perfectly comparable.
  • Missing data is not random. It mostly affects small territories and countries in conflict or isolation (Somalia, South Sudan, Eritrea, Syria, Yemen, North Korea). Some of these were dropped completely (Somalia, South Sudan, North Korea), which leaves out exactly the countries a global analysis should not ignore. This choice must be reported openly, and conclusions shouldn't be applied to those countries.
