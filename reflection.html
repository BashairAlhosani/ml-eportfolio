Critical Reflection

A critical reflection on my learning in the Machine Learning module, structured using Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper's (2001) model: What? So what? Now what?

Final Reflection: Machine Learning and Professional Development

Reflective Model

Structured using Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper's (2001) model: What? So what? Now what? The reflection covers my knowledge of the machine learning algorithms in this module, my individual contribution to the team activities, my experience as a member of a development team, and the impact on my professional and personal development.

Word count: 999 words (limit 1,000), including headings, excluding references Also submitted: as a Word document with this portfolio’s address in its title

DescriptionWhat?

Over twelve units I progressed from exploratory data analysis to neural networks and convolutional neural networks (CNNs), alongside two collaborative discussions and two projects. In Unit 6, our team (Group 5) analysed 48,870 New York Airbnb listings, predicting price with linear regression, random forest and XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) and segmenting supply with k-means. In Unit 11, I compared a support vector machine (SVM) using hand-crafted features with a CNN on CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky, 2009). Two incidents shape this reflection: carrying most of the team project, and the tutor's feedback that our report was technically sound but "relatively descriptive", with insufficient critical discussion of limitations.

AnalysisSo What?

My knowledge of machine learning algorithms

I began the module believing that the best model was simply the one with the highest score. The weekly experiments dismantled this belief. In Unit 3, a single outlier turned an unrelated dataset into an apparently strong correlation (r = 0.11 to 0.78). In Unit 5, k-means produced four neat clusters from uniformly random points, and in Unit 7 a single perceptron could never learn XOR. Each showed that an algorithm always returns an answer, and that judging whether the answer means anything is the practitioner's responsibility (Domingos, 2012).

The CNN units extended this to deep learning. The Unit 9 network reached 64.6% accuracy overall but only 42% on cats, and in Unit 10 a CNN labelled a chest X-ray "ladybug" with 53% confidence, because a softmax output cannot say "none of these". In Unit 11, cross-validation exposed an SVM that scored 99.7% on its training folds but 63.0% on validation; LIME (Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin, 2016) showed it calling a bird an airplane because of the sky behind it; and a controlled brightness test showed the more accurate CNN falling from 88% to 62% in low light while the SVM barely changed. Classical and deep models fail in different ways, so choosing between them is a question of risk, not only of accuracy. My own evaluation had flaws too: I ranked my CNN variants by test accuracy, effectively tuning on the test set, and validated the CNN on one hold-out set while cross-validating the SVM. I had not yet applied my scrutiny of models to my own methods.

My individual contribution to the team activities

Participation in our team was uneven, and in practice I carried most of the project: the data cleaning and feature engineering, the regression and clustering code, the error analysis by price band, most of the writing, the team contract and both submissions. I fixed the snapshot date to prevent data leakage, and I reported the choice of four clusters as a judgement call rather than a statistical optimum. I am proud of that work, but the feedback revealed its limits. A report built largely by one person reflected one perspective: strong on technique, weaker on the business interpretation that more voices might have challenged. The tutor also noted that the report did not identify the team members. Names were still being confirmed on submission day, and as the person assembling the final document, I should have resolved this earlier.

My experience as a member of a development team

The imbalance was frustrating and stressful, especially near the deadline. The pressure also affected the work itself: short of time, I prioritised finishing the analysis over refining its business interpretation, which is exactly where the feedback found weaknesses. Research on social loafing shows that individual effort falls when contributions are pooled and difficult to identify (Latané, Williams and Harkins, 1979), and distributed teams are prone to conflict because members lack shared context (Hinds and Bailey, 2003). Understanding this helped me respond constructively rather than personally: I kept sharing drafts and inviting the team to review and change them, and I chose not to escalate the workload imbalance. In Tuckman's (1965) terms, our team never progressed fully from forming to performing. Looking back, although I drafted a team contract, I compensated by doing the work myself instead of using it to build the structures that make contributions visible: agreed individual roles, a contribution log and short meetings with recorded actions. Completing the project nevertheless gave me confidence that I can deliver a complete machine learning analysis from start to finish.

The collaborative discussions provided the peer challenge that the team project lacked. In Discussion 2, my peers' cases exposed gaps in my framework for assessing AI writers, and revising it in my summary post taught me more than defending it would have.

ActionNow What?

Impact on my professional and personal development

I intend to apply machine learning in business and finance, and this module has changed my view of what that requires. Financial models affect people directly: AI systems used to assess creditworthiness are classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act (European Union, 2024). Such systems demand the habits I developed here: testing on unseen data, reporting results by subgroup, explaining predictions and stating limitations openly. I will document my models and data with model cards and datasheets (Mitchell et al., 2019; Gebru et al., 2021), so that decision-makers can see where a model can and cannot be trusted.

The Unit 6 feedback also stays with me. A model has value only when its results inform a business decision, so I will lead with the business question and use technical detail to support it, not replace it. I acted on this in Unit 11 by grounding my recommendation in a specific use case and the cost of each type of error.

For teamwork, I will agree roles and a contribution log at the first meeting, record decisions and actions, and raise imbalances early and openly rather than absorbing them. My professional development plan sets out these actions alongside technical goals: explainability for tabular financial data and validation methods for time-dependent data.

This module showed me that competence in machine learning lies less in building models than in questioning them, and that the same honesty applies to how I work with others.
